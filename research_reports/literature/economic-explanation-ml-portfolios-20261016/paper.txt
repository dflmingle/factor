                                    Abstract
To extend conventional model-level feature importance to economically meaningful
portfolio outcomes and decisions, we propose a local first-order attribution-
propagation framework that maps allocation-function feature exposures to local
sensitivities of sequential portfolio performance metrics. We relate the propagated
attributions to Shapley values defined directly for portfolio objectives and
derive statistical inference for Sharpe-ratio attributions. The resulting attribution-
guided feature-exposure intervention enables post-training adaptation, translating
portfolio-metric explanations into out-of-sample decisions without retraining the
underlying allocation model. We further introduce temporal stabilisation and cross-
model averaging as complementary regularisation mechanisms. Empirically, the
interventions yield statistically significant improvements in net Sharpe ratios across
different model classes.


Keywords: feature attribution, portfolio performance attribution, machine-learning
portfolios, Shapley values, statistical arbitrage, feature-exposure intervention

JEL Classification: C45, C58, G11



Introduction
As machine-learning methods are increasingly used in portfolio construction, it has
become more difficult to understand how complex allocation models transform input
features or trading signals into portfolio decisions. Model-level explanation methods
can identify which features influence model outputs, such as predictions or allocation
weights, but this is not necessarily the information most relevant to an investor.
Ultimately, investors care about realised portfolio outcomes, including returns, risk,
turnover and transaction costs. A feature that is important for determining portfolio
weights need not contribute positively to these outcomes. In particular, a signal
may improve gross portfolio performance while simultaneously generating substantial
turnover and transaction costs, so that its contribution to net performance becomes
negative.
   This distinction creates a gap between explaining a machine-learning model and
explaining the economic performance of the portfolio generated by that model.
Existing machine-learning explanation tools primarily attribute model outputs to
input features. In portfolio construction, however, model outputs are only an
intermediate stage of the investment process. Features affect allocation weights,
while changes in weights determine portfolio turnover. Allocations combined with
asset returns generate portfolio returns, and the resulting return distribution
determines mean return, volatility, and risk-adjusted performance. Once transaction
costs are introduced, turnover further determines the wedge between gross and
net performance. An economically relevant attribution framework should therefore
explain not only how features affect allocation decisions, but also how these effects
propagate through the portfolio-construction process to the performance metrics that
investors ultimately seek to improve.

   A second limitation of conventional explainability analysis is that it is
predominantly diagnostic. Feature attributions are typically computed after a model
has been trained to explain its predictions, but they are rarely used directly
to inform subsequent out-of-sample decisions. This is particularly restrictive in
portfolio construction, where the primary objective is not interpretability itself but
improved out-of-sample economic performance. Moreover, explanation methods are
often tailored to particular model classes, making it difficult to determine whether
economically useful feature information is specific to an individual allocation model
or shared across different models. In practice, portfolio managers may employ
several allocation models simultaneously, and feature contributions that are persistent
through time or common across models may provide more robust information for
subsequent investment decisions.
   Against this background, this paper addresses two related questions.

(1) How can portfolio metrics be attributed within a unified framework?
Individual portfolio metrics can in principle be attributed separately using Shapley
values or other feature-attribution methods. Such an approach, however, does not
explicitly characterise how feature contributions are transmitted across economically
linked portfolio quantities. For example, an attribution of the Sharpe ratio should
reveal whether a feature’s positive contribution arises primarily from a higher mean
return, lower portfolio volatility, or a combination of the two. Similarly, attribution
of the net Sharpe ratio should distinguish a feature’s contribution to gross risk-
adjusted performance from its effect on transaction costs through portfolio turnover.
Treating allocation weights, turnover, returns, volatility, and Sharpe ratios as separate
attribution problems obscures these economic channels.
    We therefore develop a local first-order attribution-propagation framework that
characterises the sensitivities of sequential portfolio metrics to feature exposures in
an allocation function. Starting from feature contributions to allocation weights,
the framework propagates local effects through portfolio turnover, daily portfolio
returns, mean returns, volatility, and ultimately gross and net Sharpe ratios. The
resulting attribution provides a unified decomposition of the portfolio-construction
process while retaining the relationships among its individual components. Because
the framework is local, feature contributions can be evaluated at the feature-stock-
date level and subsequently aggregated across stocks, trading dates, and evaluation
periods.
    We further establish the theoretical relationship between the propagated first-order
attributions and Shapley values defined directly for the target portfolio metrics. The
latter provide a natural benchmark for assessing whether the local approximation
correctly captures the direction and magnitude of feature contributions, but can
be substantially more computationally demanding for nonlinear portfolio objectives.
In addition, we derive statistical inference for Sharpe-ratio attributions, allowing
us to assess whether estimated feature contributions are statistically distinguishable
from sampling variation. The framework therefore provides not only an economically
structured attribution of portfolio outcomes, but also a means of evaluating the
reliability of those attributions.

(2) Can portfolio-metric attribution be translated from explanation into
improved portfolio decisions?
A trained allocation function that performs well on its training sample need not
perform equally well in a subsequent validation period or out-of-sample trading
environment. Economically consistent feature-level attribution may therefore provide
useful information for adapting feature exposures over time. Conditional on a fixed
allocation function and the available input features, local portfolio-metric attribution
can identify directions in feature exposure that are associated with improvements
or deteriorations in a target objective. If these attribution structures contain a
persistent component, information estimated before the out-of-sample trading period
may provide useful guidance regarding which trading signals should receive greater
or smaller exposure subsequently.
    This observation motivates an attribution-guided feature-exposure intervention.
We use portfolio-metric attribution to modify the effective exposure of individual
features entering the existing allocation rule. Features with persistently positive
contributions to the target objective receive greater exposure, whereas features with
persistently negative contributions receive lower exposure. The procedure therefore
constitutes a form of post-training adaptation, translating attribution information
into subsequent portfolio decisions while the underlying allocation model remains
fixed.
    The effectiveness of such an intervention depends critically on the stability of
the attribution signal. We consequently introduce two complementary regularisation
mechanisms. First, temporal stabilisation aggregates previously available attribution
information to reduce the influence of transitory feature contributions. Second, cross-
model averaging combines attribution information from different allocation models.
The latter is motivated by the possibility that feature contributions to economic
outcomes contain a common component related to persistent feature characteristics
or market conditions, beyond solely the idiosyncratic structure of a particular trained
model. Cross-model attribution information can therefore provide an additional form
of regularisation when constructing feature-exposure rules.
    The framework makes three main methodological contributions. First, it
extends feature attribution from model outputs to economically relevant portfolio
outcomes while preserving the propagation channels linking allocation weights,
turnover, returns, volatility, transaction costs, and Sharpe ratios. Second, it
connects the resulting local first-order attributions to Shapley values for the
corresponding portfolio objectives and provides statistical inference for Sharpe-
ratio feature contributions. Third, it converts attribution from a purely post
hoc diagnostic into an input to subsequent portfolio decisions through feature-
exposure interventions. Temporal stabilisation and cross-model averaging provide
complementary mechanisms for regularising these interventions without requiring
retraining of the underlying allocation model.
    We evaluate the framework in a statistical-arbitrage setting using two substantially
different machine-learning allocation functions, extreme gradient boosting (XGB) and
feed-forward neural networks (FFN). The analysis covers daily out-of-sample trading
from 2006 to 2024. Starting from efficient SHAP-based feature attributions of residual
From Economic Explanation to Performance Improvement in Machine-Learning Portfolios   5


portfolio weights, we propagate feature contributions through portfolio turnover and
gross and net portfolio-performance metrics. Our baseline net-performance analysis
incorporates transaction costs of 5 basis points per unit of turnover. We then compare
the resulting first-order portfolio-metric attributions with numerically converged
Shapley-value estimates and examine whether the attribution information available
before each trading period can improve subsequent out-of-sample portfolio decisions.
   The empirical results provide evidence that the proposed attribution framework
is both statistically reliable and economically informative. First, the local first-
order Sharpe-ratio attributions are closely aligned with estimated Shapley values.
Across hundreds of feature-year observations, the correlations between the proposed
attributions and estimated Shapley values are approximately 0.76 for XGB and
0.90 for FFN for both gross and net Sharpe ratios. Approximately 10% of feature-
year attributions are statistically significant at the 5% level for both gross and net
Sharpe ratios, indicating that a non-negligible subset of feature contributions is
distinguishable from noise.
   The attribution analysis also demonstrates that feature contributions to model
outputs and portfolio outcomes are related but economically distinct. Features
with large contributions to residual portfolio weights need not have correspondingly
large contributions to turnover or risk-adjusted performance. Some relatively short-
horizon or residual-based signals make limited contributions to residual weights but
generate substantial turnover, particularly in certain stress periods. As a result,
features that contribute positively to gross returns or the gross Sharpe ratio can
contribute negatively to net performance once trading costs are taken into account.
The propagation framework identifies these channels directly by decomposing the
path from allocation decisions to turnover, gross performance, transaction costs, and
net performance.
   We further find systematic structure in portfolio-level feature contributions across
both time and allocation models. Turnover attributions are highly persistent across
adjacent trading periods. Net Sharpe-ratio attributions display stronger temporal
persistence than gross Sharpe-ratio attributions, particularly for the higher-turnover
FFN strategy, consistent with the persistent contribution of turnover to transaction
costs. At the same time, gross and net Sharpe-ratio attributions display positive
cross-model similarity between XGB and FFN, despite substantial differences between
the underlying allocation functions. These results suggest that portfolio-performance
attributions contain an economically relevant component that extends beyond model-
specific allocation mechanisms.
   Most importantly, the attribution information is useful for subsequent out-of-
sample portfolio decisions. Using only information available prior to each trading
period, we construct feature-exposure rules based on previous-period and temporally
stabilised net Sharpe-ratio attributions, both at the model-specific level and by
averaging information across models. The resulting interventions produce statistically
significant improvements in net Sharpe ratios across the two allocation models. For
FFN, the annualised net Sharpe ratio over 2006-2024 increases from 0.5750 for the
baseline portfolio to 0.6846 using previous-year attribution information and to 0.8250
using temporally stabilised attributions. The latter improvement is accompanied by
a substantial reduction in average daily turnover, from 26.70% to 13.02%. For XGB,
6   From Economic Explanation to Performance Improvement in Machine-Learning Portfolios


stabilised cross-model attribution increases the net Sharpe ratio from approximately
0.6175 to 0.7559, with the improvement statistically significant at the 5% level.
    The economic channels underlying these improvements differ across the two
allocation models. For the higher-turnover FFN strategy, temporal stabilisation
exploits the strong persistence of turnover-related attribution and improves net
performance primarily by reducing portfolio turnover and transaction costs. For the
lower-turnover XGB strategy, cross-model averaging provides additional attribution
information from FFN and improves performance primarily through an increase in the
gross Sharpe ratio while turnover remains relatively stable. Thus, the same portfolio-
objective attribution framework can improve net performance through different
economic channels depending on the characteristics of the underlying allocation
model.
    Additional evidence confirms that net factor-adjusted alpha also improves using
net Sharpe ratio attribution information. Under the stabilised cross-model feature-
exposure rule, the annualised net factor-adjusted alpha increases from 1.19% to 1.85%
for XGB and from 1.12% to 2.23% for FFN. In both cases, the attribution-guided
portfolios exhibit statistically significant net alphas even though the corresponding
baseline net alphas are statistically insignificant. Moreover, the attribution-guided
interventions remain effective across a broad range of transaction-cost assumptions.
Attributions estimated under higher transaction-cost rates tend to perform better in
higher-cost out-of-sample environments, particularly for the higher-turnover strategy,
while the benefits for the lower-turnover strategy are less sensitive to the assumed
cost level.
    Overall, the results show that portfolio-level explainability can serve a role
beyond model interpretation. By characterising how individual features affect
economically relevant portfolio objectives and identifying persistent components of
those contributions, attribution information can be transformed into implementable
adjustments to subsequent portfolio decisions. The framework therefore connects
explainability, statistical inference, and portfolio choice without requiring the
underlying machine-learning allocation model to be retrained.
    The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section 1 reviews the related
literature. Section 2 presents the methodology, including the statistical-arbitrage
portfolio construction, the local first-order attribution-propagation framework,
statistical inference for Sharpe-ratio attributions, and attribution-guided feature-
exposure interventions. Section 3 presents the empirical results, including baseline
portfolio performance, feature contributions to allocation decisions and portfolio
metrics, validation of attribution reliability, cross-model attribution similarity and
temporal persistence, and out-of-sample performance improvements from feature-
exposure interventions. Section 5 summarises and concludes this work.




1. Related Literature
This paper relates to three strands of literature, including machine learning in
asset pricing and portfolio construction, explainable machine learning and feature
attribution, and portfolio-performance attribution and economic feature importance.
From Economic Explanation to Performance Improvement in Machine-Learning Portfolios   7


Machine learning in asset pricing and portfolio construction
A growing literature applies machine-learning methods to return prediction and
asset-pricing models. Gu et al. (2020) document substantial gains in predictive
and economic performance from flexible nonlinear methods, particularly tree-based
models and neural networks. Freyberger et al. (2020) similarly show that nonlinear
methods help identify the incremental information contained in firm characteristics.
A related strand uses machine learning to model latent factors and conditional risk
exposures. Kelly et al. (2019) introduce instrumented principal component analysis, in
which observable characteristics determine time-varying factor exposures, while Gu
et al. (2021) extend this framework using autoencoder neural networks to capture
nonlinearities and interactions. Giglio et al. (2022) provide a broader review of
machine-learning approaches to expected returns, factor structures, risk premia, and
stochastic discount factors.
    A complementary literature applies machine learning directly to portfolio
construction. Lim et al. (2019) introduce Deep Momentum Networks, which jointly
learn trading signals and position sizing while directly optimising the Sharpe ratio and
accounting for turnover. Zhang et al. (2020) extend direct Sharpe-ratio optimisation
to multi-asset portfolio allocation, and Zhang et al. (2021) develop a general end-to-
end framework that maps inputs directly into portfolio weights under economically
relevant objectives and constraints. Poh et al. (2021) formulate cross-sectional
portfolio construction as a learning-to-rank problem, explicitly learning pairwise and
listwise relations across securities. More recently, Guijarro-Ordonez et al. (2025)
combine residual-based statistical arbitrage with deep-learning signal extraction and
neural-network portfolio allocation. These studies show that machine-learning models
can move beyond intermediate return forecasting to target economically relevant
portfolio objectives directly.
    These studies establish the value of machine learning for return prediction and
portfolio construction. Our paper addresses a complementary question, namely how
individual input features contribute to the realised economic performance of the
resulting portfolio once a machine-learning allocation model has been trained.

Explainable machine learning and feature attribution
Our methodology builds on the literature on Shapley values and explainable
machine learning. Shapley (1953) introduced the Shapley value as an allocation
rule based on the average marginal contribution of each participant across possible
coalitions. Lundberg and Lee (2017) adapt this principle to machine learning
through SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations), which decomposes individual
model predictions into feature-level contributions. Subsequent work develops efficient
attribution methods for specific model classes. Lundberg et al. (2020) provide exact
SHAP algorithms for tree-based models and introduce SHAP interaction values
for measuring pairwise feature interactions. For neural networks, Shrikumar et
al. (2017) propose DeepLIFT, while Sundararajan et al. (2017) develop Integrated
Gradients. More broadly, Molnar (2025) surveys a wide range of model-specific and
model-agnostic approaches to interpretable machine learning.
    A related literature studies interpretability in financial machine-learning models.
Wood et al. (2021) use attention to assess variable and temporal importance in the
8   From Economic Explanation to Performance Improvement in Machine-Learning Portfolios


Momentum Transformer, while Tan et al. (2023) apply SHAP to interpret spatio-
temporal features in momentum strategies. An important issue in feature attribution
is dependence among predictors. Aas et al. (2021) show that independence-
based implementations of Kernel SHAP can produce misleading explanations when
predictors are correlated and propose conditional approaches that account for feature
dependence. This concern is particularly relevant in financial applications, where
trading signals and firm characteristics can be strongly related. We assess the
reliability of the proposed portfolio-level attributions using Shapley-value benchmarks
and separately examine interaction effects. The interaction structure underlying
model predictions need not coincide with that governing economically relevant
portfolio outcomes, such as the Sharpe ratio.
    The distinction between model-level importance and economic contribution is
central to our analysis. Standard feature-importance measures, such as split-gain
importance in boosted trees, quantify the relevance of a feature for model fitting but
do not indicate whether its effect is beneficial for the investor’s objective. SHAP-type
methods provide signed local contributions and therefore offer a more informative
starting point. Nevertheless, attribution to a model output is not equivalent to
attribution to portfolio performance.
    Our framework extends model-level attribution to economically relevant portfolio
outcomes. Starting from feature contributions to the allocation function, we propagate
local effects through portfolio weights, turnover, returns, volatility, and gross and net
Sharpe ratios. The objective is therefore not only to explain how a feature affects
a model output, but also to identify how that effect is transmitted to the portfolio-
performance metric relevant to the investor.

Portfolio performance attribution and economic feature importance
Our paper is most closely related to research that evaluates investment inputs
directly in terms of portfolio outcomes. Moehle et al. (2022) apply Shapley values to
portfolio-performance attribution and decompose achieved portfolio performance into
contributions from investment-style components. Our setting differs primarily in the
object and granularity of attribution. Instead of focusing on broad investment-style
components, we study individual predictive features within nonlinear machine-
learning allocation functions. The proposed framework generates local feature
contributions at the stock-date level and propagates them through sequential portfolio
metrics before aggregation across stocks and time. This allows us to identify not
only whether a feature contributes positively or negatively to a portfolio objective,
but also the economic channel through which the contribution arises. In particular,
the contribution to the net Sharpe ratio can be decomposed into effects on gross
performance and transaction costs through turnover.
    Transaction costs could be central to the distinction between predictive and
economic importance. DeMiguel et al. (2020) show that trading costs materially
affect the economic value of firm characteristics in portfolio choice. Jensen et al.
(2026) further develop the implementable efficient frontier and introduce an economic
feature-importance measure within a machine-learning portfolio framework. Their
analysis shows that transaction costs can substantially alter the economic value
assigned to individual signals.
From Economic Explanation to Performance Improvement in Machine-Learning Portfolios      9


    Our approach is complementary to these works. Their framework incorporates
trading costs directly into the portfolio-learning problem and evaluates feature
importance within an economically optimised allocation rule. We instead take an
already-trained allocation function, optimised for the net Sharpe ratio, as given
and measure the local contribution of its individual features to subsequent portfolio
objectives. When these attribution structures exhibit sufficient persistence, they can
be used to adjust feature exposures for next trading period without re-estimating the
underlying allocation model. The resulting intervention therefore provides a form of
post-training adaptation.
    Related work also studies interpretable portfolio models directly. Bell et al. (2026)
apply Explainable Boosting Machines to corporate bond return prediction, providing
interpretable feature effects and interactions and highlighting the economic value
of model transparency. Our approach addresses a different problem by retaining
flexible allocation functions, whether inherently interpretable or not, while developing
a common portfolio-level attribution framework that can be applied across model
architectures.
    Overall, the existing literature provides methods for predicting returns with
machine learning, explaining model outputs, and attributing economic importance
of investment signals at a global level. Our paper connects these strands by
tracing local feature effects from machine-learning allocation models through the
portfolio-construction process and by using the resulting attribution information for
subsequent portfolio decisions. The framework links allocation weights, turnover,
returns, volatility, and gross and net Sharpe ratios within a unified first-order
attribution system, relates these attributions to Shapley-value benchmarks and
statistical inference, and uses temporally stabilised and cross-model attribution
signals to construct feature-exposure interventions without retraining the underlying
allocation model.



2. Methodology
The methodology proceeds in three stages. First, we describe the construction
of the arbitrage portfolios. Second, we introduce the local first-order objective-
attribution framework, establish its relationship with Shapley values, and describe
how feature contributions propagate from the allocation-model output, represented
by residual portfolio weights, to economically relevant portfolio metrics, including
turnover and the Sharpe ratio. We also derive statistical inference for assessing the
significance of Sharpe-ratio attributions. Finally, we present the attribution-guided
feature-exposure intervention framework and introduce temporal stabilisation and
cross-model averaging as complementary regularisation mechanisms for translating
portfolio-metric attributions into out-of-sample trading decisions.

ensemble to be trained directly with respect to a portfolio-level, non-additive objective
such as the negative net Sharpe ratio. The gradient of the portfolio loss with respect
to the model outputs is used to construct the boosting updates. We therefore employ
XGB as the representative gradient-boosted tree allocation model. Model complexity
is controlled through the structure of the individual trees, including restrictions on
the maximum tree depth and the minimum child weight.

2.2. Portfolio performance explainability
This section presents a local first-order attribution-propagation framework for
explaining machine-learning-based arbitrage portfolios. The framework characterizes
the local sensitivities of sequential portfolio metrics with respect to feature exposures
in the allocation function.
   The framework consists of two layers. The first attributes the residual allocation
weights generated by the machine-learning allocation model to the input features.
The second propagates these allocation-level attributions through the portfolio
construction and performance evaluation pipeline to economically meaningful
portfolio metrics, including daily excess return, mean return, volatility, turnover,
gross Sharpe ratio, and net Sharpe ratio. By exploiting the differentiable structure
of the portfolio-performance pipeline, the framework efficiently derives feature-level
contribution time series for each portfolio metric. It therefore establishes a unified
attribution chain from input signals to allocation decisions and ultimately to realised
portfolio performance.
   The proposed framework offers three key advantages. First, it provides a unified
attribution across the entire portfolio-performance pipeline, explicitly revealing how
feature contributions propagate from allocation decisions to downstream portfolio
metrics. Second, the propagated attributions exhibit strong empirical agreement
with the corresponding estimated Shapley values, providing evidence that the
proposed local first-order attribution captures the dominant attribution structure
of finite-difference attribution. Third, the framework assigns an explicit economic
interpretation to feature attribution by linking local feature contributions to feature
exposures. For portfolio objectives whose attribution structures remain sufficiently
stable over time, these local sensitivities can be used to guide post-training feature-
exposure adjustment, potentially improving out-of-sample portfolio performance
without retraining the allocation model.

2.2.2. Relation to Shapley attribution
Direct Shapley values measure the realised change in the target portfolio objective
when the information contained in a feature is added to a coalition of other features,
averaged over all possible coalitions. The Shapley value can be decomposed into the
main effect of each feature and the pairwise interaction effects between features. They
therefore provide a model-agnostic benchmark for evaluating whether the propagated
local first-order attributions identify economically meaningful feature effects.
    Although direct Shapley values are theoretically appealing, they are
computationally expensive because they require evaluating a large number of feature
coalitions. They also provide only an aggregate attribution to the final objective and
do not naturally reveal the intermediate economic channels through which a feature
affects performance. For example, a direct Shapley value for the net Sharpe ratio
does not indicate whether a positive contribution arises from higher gross return,
lower volatility, lower turnover, or a combination of these channels. In contrast,
the proposed propagation framework produces a complete attribution chain from
allocation weights to turnover, return, volatility, and Sharpe-ratio components. It
also yields local contribution time series at the stock-time level, which are not readily
available from direct Shapley evaluation.
    We additionally compute Shapley interaction effects for portfolio objectives such as
the Sharpe ratio. The interaction component measures the extent to which the joint
contribution of two features differs from the sum of their individual contributions.
If interaction effects are small relative to main effects, feature contributions are
approximately separable. This provides support for analysing and adjusting feature
exposures individually. For different target metrics, the interaction effects can differ
even within the same feature universe.

2.3. Explainability-driven feature exposure intervention
The attribution framework can also be used to guide out-of-sample feature exposure
interventions. Throughout the paper, feature intervention refers to changing the
informational exposure of a feature. Since input features are standardised, setting
a feature to zero corresponds to its average or signal-neutral state. The resulting
intervention therefore measures the economic value of feature information relative to
a neutral benchmark.
   Traditional Shapley values and SHAP explanations compare the effect of including
versus excluding feature information. Feature exposure intervention generalises this
From Economic Explanation to Performance Improvement in Machine-Learning Portfolios 23


idea by allowing feature information to be scaled continuously. A feature can be
partially suppressed, left unchanged, or amplified before being passed into the
trained allocation model. Importantly, the model parameters are not retrained. The
intervention therefore isolates the portfolio effect of changing feature exposure while
holding the learned allocation rule fixed.
    The proposed intervention framework is based on two key considerations. First,
within the given feature universe, the intervention is most informative when the
main effects of individual features are expected to dominate their interaction effects
when feature exposures are modified separately. Second, feature contributions
should exhibit sufficient temporal persistence such that lagged contribution estimates
remain informative for future trading periods. These properties are evaluated in our
empirical study. Evidence in support of these properties helps explain the empirical
effectiveness of the attribution-guided feature-exposure intervention and provides
practical guidance for its implementation.
    All feature exposure multipliers are computed using only contribution information
available before the beginning of the trading period in which they are applied,
thereby avoiding look-ahead bias. The contribution signal may use only the sign
of the estimated contribution, the rank of the contribution across features, or the
full contribution magnitude. Exposure multipliers can be constructed either from
the most recent estimation window or from recursively accumulated contribution
information to improve temporal stability. Furthermore, the exposure rule can
be model-specific or cross-model. In the model-specific case, FFN and XGBoost
feature contributions generate separate exposure adjustments. In the cross-model
case, feature contribution signals are averaged across models to extract economic
information that is less sensitive to a particular model class. This design allows
us to distinguish model-specific attribution patterns from more persistent economic
feature effects. Stabilisation and cross-model averaging can serve as complementary
regularisation mechanisms for the use of attribution information.

3. Empirical Analysis
In the empirical analysis, we first evaluate the arbitrage trading performance of
the baseline portfolios constructed using extreme gradient boosting (XGB) and
feedforward neural networks (FFN) as allocation functions. We then examine the
portfolio-level metrics and their attributions, assess the statistical significance of
the Sharpe-ratio attributions, and evaluate the extent to which the proposed local
first-order attributions align with estimated Shapley values. For attribution-guided
feature-exposure interventions, we provide evidence on the cross-model similarity of
portfolio-level attributions and their persistence over time. We subsequently evaluate
the economic significance of the resulting improvements in out-of-sample Sharpe
ratios and assess the robustness of the feature-exposure strategies across alternative
transaction-cost assumptions.


3.1. Arbitrage trading performance
For the empirical study, we use daily U.S. equity data from October 2000 through
December 2024. The sample consists of a universe of 346 large-capitalisation and
liquid U.S. stocks, which reduces concerns about trading frictions and market impact.
Our empirical analysis focuses primarily on within-universe comparisons between the
baseline portfolios and their corresponding attribution-guided strategies to assess the
incremental economic value of portfolio-objective attributions.
   For the factor models, we use the Fama-French 5-factor specifications. Factor data
are obtained from the Kenneth French Data Library. The 5-factor model includes the
market, size, value, investment, and profitability factors. These factors are projected
to our universe for constructing the real tradable portfolios. Stock factor loadings are
estimated daily via rolling regressions of returns on the chosen factor set, using a
60-day window. The out-of-sample Residuals are then computed for each stock. This
procedure yields daily residual series beginning in January 2001.
   Given these residuals, we construct trading signals within a rolling-window
framework. A local lookback window of L = 30 trading days is used to construct the
signals, while the signal-extraction and allocation models are estimated over a five-
year rolling window comprising a four-year training period and a one-year validation
period. For computational efficiency, the models are re-estimated every 252 trading
days using the most recent five years of data. The resulting out-of-sample trading
period spans January 2006 to December 2024.

directly determines transaction costs, the economic contribution of a feature depends
not only on its effect on portfolio allocation but also on its impact on trading activity.
This distinction is especially important when comparing gross and net portfolio
performance, as the inclusion of transaction costs can significantly alter the economic
outcomes.

    Moreover, the divergence between gross and net Sharpe ratio feature contributions

conducted separately for gross and net Sharpe ratios.

Accordingly, a close alignment between the estimated main effects and the full
Shapley values indicates that the interaction component does not substantially
alter the feature-level contribution identified by the main effects. In particular,
strong directional agreement suggests that the interaction terms generally refine the
magnitude of the feature contributions rather than systematically reversing their

feature coalitions. When interaction and higher-order effects do not materially alter
the contribution direction, the Shapley estimates therefore provide a useful external
benchmark for assessing whether our estimated local first-order attribution captures
economically consistent feature-level information.

3.3.2. Trading performance with feature-exposure interventions
The preceding analysis provides evidence that validation-period net Sharpe-ratio
attributions contain economically relevant information about the subsequent out-
of-sample attribution structure in section 3.3.1 and that Sharpe-ratio attributions
exhibit a common component across XGB and FFN in section 3.2.5. We now
examine whether these attribution relationships can be translated into improvements
in realised out-of-sample portfolio performance. Following the intervention framework
in Section 2.3, we use net Sharpe-ratio attributions estimated prior to each trading
period, based on a transaction-cost rate of c = 0.0005, to determine feature exposures
for the subsequent out-of-sample period.
    For each allocation model, we consider four attribution-guided exposure rules:
(i) model-specific attributions without stabilisation; (ii) stabilised model-specific
attributions; (iii) cross-model averaged attributions without stabilisation; and (iv)
stabilised cross-model averaged attributions. We use the magnitude transformation,
which preserves both the sign and magnitude of the attribution, and set the
intervention-strength parameter to η = 0.5, with feature exposures constrained to
0 ≤ λ ≤ 3. All exposure adjustments are therefore constructed using information
available prior to the corresponding out-of-sample trading period.
    Table 7 reports the resulting portfolio performance. The attribution-guided
interventions improve the net Sharpe ratio relative to the corresponding baseline
portfolio under all eight model-rule combinations, with statistically significant
improvements in seven of the eight cases. Figures 14 and 15 provide the corresponding
time-series evidence. Figure 14 reports cumulative gross and net excess returns under
the four intervention rules, while Figure 15 reports the associated turnover dynamics.

4. Conclusion
To translate model explainability into actionable portfolio decisions, we develop
a local first-order attribution-propagation framework that measures the sensitivity
of sequential portfolio metrics to feature exposures in the allocation function. By
explicitly linking feature-level contributions to economically interpretable exposure
adjustments, the framework enables attribution-guided post-training adaptation
without requiring retraining of the underlying allocation model. Empirically, these
feature-exposure interventions generate statistically significant improvements in out-
of-sample performance when the net Sharpe ratio is used as the target portfolio
objective.
   A central implication of our analysis is that model-level attribution to allocation
weights does not map directly into attribution to economically relevant portfolio
outcomes. Features that are influential for portfolio weights need not be those
that contribute most positively to risk-adjusted performance after turnover and
transaction costs are taken into account. For both gross and net Sharpe
ratios, the proposed local first-order attributions exhibit strong agreement with
the corresponding estimated Shapley values, while Sharpe-ratio attributions are
statistically significant for a non-negligible subset of feature-year observations.
   To improve the robustness of attribution-guided exposure adjustment, temporal
stabilisation and cross-model averaging can serve as regularisation mechanisms. The
results suggest that cumulative attribution structures exhibit greater persistence
over time, particularly for turnover and net Sharpe ratio. Cross-model attribution
and exposure rules also remain effective, suggesting the presence of model-agnostic
economic components in feature contributions that depend more fundamentally on
signal characteristics and market conditions. Together, these findings demonstrate
how portfolio-level attribution can move beyond explaining model decisions toward
economically meaningful and actionable post-training portfolio adaptation.

Limitations and future research
Our analysis also suggests several directions for future research. First, the proposed
local first-order objective attribution focuses on the marginal contribution of
individual features and does not explicitly account for interaction effects when
multiple feature exposures are adjusted simultaneously around the baseline trading
strategy. For the feature universe considered in this study, the first-order attribution
captures the dominant contribution structure and provides useful guidance for
From Economic Explanation to Performance Improvement in Machine-Learning Portfolios 53


feature-exposure adjustment, leading to significant improvements in out-of-sample net
Sharpe ratios. However, with hundreds of potentially correlated features, interactions
may become economically important, and simultaneous exposure adjustments may no
longer be well approximated by individual first-order effects. Extending the framework
to incorporate higher-order attribution and interaction effects would therefore be
valuable for high-dimensional feature spaces.
   Moreover, feature attribution and interaction structures may be regime dependent.
A feature that improves portfolio performance in one regime may become less
informative or even detrimental in another. For example, signals that exploit
transient arbitrage opportunities may generate higher turnover and become more
valuable during volatile periods, but reduce the net Sharpe ratio in stable markets
once transaction costs are considered. For interaction effects, complementarities
among important signals may become stronger during periods of market stress.
This motivates regime-dependent feature-exposure policies that condition exposure
adjustments on prevailing market states. Incorporating regime-dependent first- and
higher-order attribution into feature-exposure calibration therefore could represent a
promising extension of the proposed framework.

B.3. Information of feature contributions
We investigate which aspects of feature-contribution information are most useful
for feature-exposure interventions. While the main analysis uses the magnitude of
feature contributions, we further consider interventions based on their sign and rank
to assess the information content of alternative attribution signals. Tables 12 reports
the corresponding trading performance.
    The results show that interventions based on the sign, rank, and magnitude
of feature contributions generate remarkably similar portfolio performance. This
suggests that the direction of feature contributions constitutes an important
component of the attribution signal for feature-exposure interventions, while
information about their relative ranking and magnitude provides incremental benefits.
    Consistent with the magnitude-based results, the sources of performance
improvement differ across allocation models. For XGB, cross-model averaging
provides additional attribution information that primarily improves the gross Sharpe
ratio, while portfolio turnover remains relatively stable. For FFN, the improvement
is driven mainly by a substantial reduction in turnover, which raises the net Sharpe
ratio despite a lower gross Sharpe ratio. Stabilised feature-exposure interventions
consistently outperform their unstabilised counterparts for FFN.
    Finally, after combining temporal stabilisation with cross-model averaging, the
XGB and FFN strategies exhibit similar portfolio characteristics in terms of gross
Sharpe ratio, turnover, and net Sharpe ratio. This convergence provides further
evidence that stabilised cross-model attributions capture common and economically
meaningful information about feature efficiency.
